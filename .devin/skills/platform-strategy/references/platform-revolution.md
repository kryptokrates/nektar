# Platform Revolution (Parker, Van Alstyne, Choudary) — deep reference notes

## 1. Open-source & openness (ch.7 + ch.3, 8, 11)

- Definition of openness (Eisenmann/Parker/Van Alstyne 2009): open = no restrictions on participation in development, commercialization, or use; or restrictions (technical standards, license fees) are reasonable and non-discriminatory, applied uniformly. "Closed" includes onerous participation rules and rents so high participant margins become unsustainable — it's a spectrum, not binary.
- Openness is "one of the most complex and most critical" platform decisions — affects usage, developer participation, monetization, regulation. Jobs got it wrong in the 1980s (closed Macintosh lost to licensed Windows) and right in the 2000s (opened iPhone OS to developers, iTunes on Windows).
- More open = more fragmented + harder to monetize + harder to control IP, but more innovation. Jobs's "fragmented vs. integrated" framing subtly skewed toward closed.
- Too-open failure — Wikipedia: the "Murder of Meredith Kercher" entry was edited 8,000+ times by 1,000+ largely anti-Knox editors; Jimmy Wales intervened and was himself attacked. Open utopia (users self-police) failed; needed layered curation.
- Too-closed AND too-open simultaneously — Myspace: kept out extension developers (DeWolfe: "We should have picked 5 to 10 key features and let other people innovate on everything else") while self-serve ads let porn in and triggered state AG investigations. Facebook opened to dot-com users (2006), then Facebook Platform (May 2007) → 7,000 outside apps by November; Myspace opened Feb 2008, too late — Facebook passed it April 2008.
- Three kinds of openness decisions: (a) manager/sponsor participation, (b) developer participation, (c) user participation.
- Manager vs. sponsor, in full: the **manager** organizes and controls producer/consumer interactions, sits closest to customers and outside developers → day-to-day influence. The **sponsor** controls overall architecture, the underlying IP (software code), and allocation of rights → greater legal/economic power over long-term strategy. Same entity in Facebook, Uber, eBay, Airbnb, Alibaba.
- Four manager/sponsor models (= patterns of openness): **proprietary** (one firm manages + sponsors — Apple Mac/iOS; most control, most closed) | **licensing** (one sponsor, many managers — Google sponsors stock Android; Samsung/Sony/LG/Motorola/Huawei/Amazon licensed to manage the interface) | **joint venture** (one manager, many sponsors — Orbitz launched by major airlines vs. Travelocity; CareerBuilder by three newspaper groups) | **shared** (many sponsors, many managers — Linux: sponsors IBM, Intel, HP, Fujitsu, NEC, Oracle, Samsung; managers TiVo, Roomba, Ubuntu, Qualcomm).
- Models migrate — Visa: BankAmericard proprietary (1958, BofA) → joint venture of banks (1970s) → freestanding proprietary (2007). Multi-sponsor committees have divergent goals and are inherently inefficient → spin-off for nimbleness.
- Standard won, sponsor lost — JVC/VHS vs. Sony/Betamax: Betamax technically superior, but JVC's licensing model enlisted many manufacturers → volume → lower prices → more consumers → more studio titles → feedback loop. VHS won the market; **JVC itself earned only modest, non-lasting profits** — winning the standard ≠ capturing its value.
- Proprietary win must be fast — Sony Blu-ray beat Toshiba HD-DVD (leveraged PS3's installed base), then streaming made the victory irrelevant. If you fight a standards war for sole control, win it before the next technology supersedes yours.
- Openness + hardware economics — Wintel: Microsoft gave SDKs away (Apple charged $10,000), IBM lost the hardware standard (partly via regulatory action) → huge dev pool + cheap PCs dominated ~20 years. Yet single-sponsor Apple's unified vision yields more elegant design — and Apple more profitable than any single Wintel firm.
- Android/AOSP — the central open-source caution: open multi-vendor innovation took ~80% smartphone share by 2014 (iOS ~15%), but AOSP doesn't channel traffic to Google services → **zero revenue/data from AOSP forks** (Amazon Kindle Fire, Xiaomi). Google's recapture: can't close the OSS code, so it closed the layer above — proprietary Google apps (search, music, calendar, keyboard, camera), Google Play license gating, Open Handset Alliance. Ron Amadeo: apps using Google APIs break on non-Google AOSP; developers choose Google APIs for reach → de facto dependence on Google's license. **Lesson: when the code must stay open, capture value via control of critical functions/services on top of it.**
- Direction of change is bounded: a proprietary platform can only become more open; a fully shared platform (Linux) can only become more closed. Platforms typically launch executing all processes internally (no partners will invest) and open out over time — curation must evolve from employees → algorithms → decentralized user curation (YouTube).
- Three developer classes: **core developers** (employed by platform co; build the core interaction — Airbnb's search, payments, insurance contracts, identity verification) | **extension developers** (outsiders adding features, extracting a slice of value they create — iTunes app makers; Airbnb photography service, Pillow/Airenvy, Urban Bellhop, Guesthop) | **data aggregators** (licensed to vacuum user/interaction data, resell for targeting — Target's pregnancy-prediction algorithm).
- Extension-developer openness tradeoff (Airbnb): too closed → lose valuable services; too open → low-quality providers tarnish the platform + oversupply shrinks each provider's profits and incentive to customize. The **API is the control point**; Airbnb's API is not open to all — a deliberate middle way.
- API as growth lever — Amazon: ~33 open APIs + 300 mashups vs. Walmart's 1; Amazon's market cap passed Walmart's June 2015. Salesforce: 50% of revenue via APIs. Expedia: 90%.
- Tiered API access = openness price discrimination — The Guardian "open in / open out": **Keyless** (headlines, metadata, info architecture — free, no rev-share), **Approved** (registered devs reprint full articles with restrictions, ad revenue shared), **Bespoke** (unlimited use, for a fee). 2,000 developers signed up in year one.
- What to open vs. own: own/control apps that are a **primary source of user value** (Apple owns preloaded apps; bought SRI for Siri). Don't own ephemeral micro-value (YouTube owns distribution tech, not videos — "Gangnam Style" is a tiny, fast-decaying fraction).
- Ownership rule 1: an extension app that **could become a platform in its own right** → own it or replace it with a platform-controlled version — Apple Maps vs. Google Maps (maps/location could siphon merchant revenue; Apple ate public humiliation — nurseries classified as airports, travelers stranded in the Australian desert, Tim Cook apology — to kill the dependency).
- Ownership rule 2: functionality **reinvented by multiple extension developers and broadly adopted** → acquire/standardize it into an open API (video/audio playback, photo editing, cut-and-paste, voice commands). Absorb when competition has already commoditized it (see Cisco/Jouret under Metrics).
- User participation: goal is maximum **high-quality** content → no platform chooses absolute openness. Curation = **screening** (who gets in) + **feedback** (shaping behavior after entry), driven by reputation. Human moderators don't scale → user-driven software curation (Wikipedia's VandalProof, tagging, earned blocking privileges; Facebook flagging; Uber/Airbnb two-way ratings). Errors both directions: loose → offensive content; tight → breast-cancer education blocked as porn.
- Rent check: too-closed = excessive/arbitrary fees → partners refuse platform-specific investments. Too-open risk: an extension developer inserts itself between platform and users and can displace the platform — SAP+ADP: ADP's own customer relationships could make it the platform host.
- Open source requires modular architecture — MacCormack/Baldwin (Management Science 2006): a commercial firm releasing code as open source had to invest heavily to decompose the integral system into modules; distributed volunteers can't maintain an unpartitioned codebase.
- Openness as governance — Bitcoin/blockchain (ch.8): open-source code + distributed ledger = a platform where not even Nakamoto can rewrite the rules to favor a participant — the only design in the book that addresses monopoly power **of the platform itself**. Pressures platforms whose monetization is pure gatekeeping (2–4% payment rakes "hard pressed to justify").
- Openness as regulatory transparency — Stallman (ch.11): free/open code lets anyone inspect what it does; abuse (spying, fraud, data misappropriation) gets exposed and corrected. Analogue to Grossman's Regulation 2.0 (open-data API for public audit).
- Book's open-source usage inventory: Linux (shared model; also Cisco AXP base), Android/AOSP, Java (Sun v. Microsoft "forking" suits 1997/2002; Android's 2015 open/proprietary split as parallel), Apache (the free web server that killed Netscape's free-browser→paid-server model), Wikipedia, Bitcoin. **Red Hat, GitHub, MySQL do not appear.**

## 2. Launch detail (ch.5)

- Chicken-or-egg: neither side joins until the other does. PayPal's sequence: frictionless signup (email + credit card vs. multi-round verification) → $10 signup + $10 referral ($20/customer, 7–10% daily growth, zero revenue — Thiel flags it as unstable) → designed incentives to tip sign-ups into _transacting_ (the $10 sits in an account — "user commitment was more important than user acquisition") → feedback loops (buyers insist on PayPal → sellers sign up → logos recruit buyers → seller referral fees) → eBay bots that bought goods insisting on PayPal (and relisted the goods — covered both sides, no warehousing). 100K→1M users in 3 months. eBay's Billpoint (with Wells Fargo) failed — late launch plus eBay squelched deals that would have promoted Billpoint off-eBay; eBay bought PayPal for $1.4B (Oct 2002) when 70% of auctions accepted it.
- Pull beats push: push marketing relies on owned channels + awareness, which works under scarcity; in abundance, awareness ≠ adoption. Design pull incentives **baked into the platform and organically tied to its interactions**. Push still works occasionally (Instagram's iTunes #1 feature slot; Twitter's SXSW screens).
- Incumbent advantage is partly illusory: resources, value chains, customer bases exist, but annual/quarterly metabolisms can't match network-speed markets.
- Position against rivals' value proposition, not the market average: YouTube (first democratic video platform — producer-first: upload contests, embeddable off-platform player, courted Myspace indie bands with one-click Flash upload, ad-revenue partner status → seeded content, a curation dynamic, producer-driven consumer pull, and producer lock-in) | Megaupload (late-mover → consumer-first seeding with internally sourced pirated/porn content → traction **but lawsuits and negative publicity — the failure mode**) | Vimeo (producer-first anyway, beating YouTube on infrastructure — HD playback, better embeddable player — for the neglected pro segment).
- **1. Follow-the-rabbit** — convert a proven pipeline/product into a platform: Amazon opened its retail pipeline → Marketplace; Intel + NTT demoed wireless economics (Intel coined the term). Limit: needs an existing successful business.
- **2. Piggyback** — stage value units on someone else's user base: PayPal on eBay; Justdial borrowed yellow-page listings + feet-on-street collection → phone directory → merchant subscribers → IPO; Craigslist-scraping startups pass leads then invite the merchant; YouTube rode Myspace's indie-band wave. Failure mode: the host fights back (eBay/Billpoint) or cuts access.
- **3. Seeding** — the platform acts as first producer and sets the quality culture: Android's $5M developer prizes across ten app categories; Adobe arranged IRS tax forms in PDF (borrowed value units, instant market); PayPal's eBay bots (simulated units); dating sites' fake profiles; Reddit's founders posted via fake profiles to seed the content culture (later: racist-content controversies); Quora editors asked and answered until users took over. Failure mode: fake traction is deceptive and can seed the wrong culture.
- **4. Marquee** — incentivize the make-or-break user set: console makers' sweetheart deals for Electronic Arts (no platform survives without EA titles); Microsoft bought Bungie and made Halo an Xbox exclusive (purchase = exclusive seeds); PayPal's cash to shoppers (marquee can be consumers); Swiss Post gave away iPads to convert mail holdouts to its digital platform.
- **5. Single-side** — standalone utility for one user set, then open the second side: OpenTable's restaurant seating-management software → consumer booking + lead fees; redBus's bus-operator inventory system; Delicious's solo bookmark storage → social sharing at critical mass.
- **6. Producer evangelism** — producers bring their own customers: Kickstarter/Indiegogo creators haul in backers; Skillshare/Udemy sign influential teachers who enroll students; Clarity experts' booking widget converts their followers; Mercateo's twist — "bring us your customers and you get last-word bidding, but only for customers you bring" (races suppliers against each other).
- **7. Big bang** — push-triggered simultaneous on-boarding in concentrated time+space: Twitter at SXSW 2007 ($11K for hallway flat-panels; text "join sxsw" to 40404 → tweets on screens → 20K→60K tweets/day, tripled usage, Web Award); Foursquare SXSW 2009; Tinder's USC frat-party launch (2012). Failure mode: needs a rare concentrated venue; SXSW is now too crowded to be heard.
- **8. Micromarket** — start in a tiny market already interacting: Facebook at Harvard — 500 signups in a socially dense community = instant critical mass (Friendster/Myspace had network-effect lock-in; scattered signups would never interact). Expansion created unconnected per-campus nodes until **cross-campus friend connections** linked them — solving chicken-or-egg once, not per campus. Stack Overflow: programming category → cooking → community-voted topics.
- Underlying techniques behind the eight: staging value creation (Huffington Post hired writers to seed posts); designing the platform to attract one side (OpenTable tools); simultaneous on-boarding (Facebook).
- Viral-growth mechanics — four elements (disease metaphor: host/germs/medium/recipient): **sender** = user spreading _their own_ value unit (not word-of-mouth about the platform) for fun/fame/fulfillment/fortune; **value unit** = must be _spreadable_ — starts an external interaction or completes an incomplete one (Quora's unanswered question, SurveyMonkey survey); confidential-document platforms can't go viral; **external network** = Facebook for Instagram/Zynga/Slide, Craigslist for Airbnb, email for OpenTable — LinkedIn chose the harder Outlook integration over the Hotmail-invite hack to own the business niche; external networks later impose restrictions (Facebook capped game-app virality) and invite-fatigue sets in; **recipient** = responds if the unit is relevant, then becomes sender — managers can't control units but can nudge (Instagram editing tools, specific hashtags #vwbus) and attach a call-to-action (Hotmail: "P.S. I love you. Get your FREE email at Hotmail").
- Instagram: 100M+ users in <2 years **without a single marketing manager** — every use of the app was an instance of marketing; users became senders via Facebook. (Hipstamatic stayed save-only.)
- Inorganic spread incentives must not be cash — Dropbox gives free storage to both sender and recipient (cash rewards become a drain at viral scale; contrast PayPal's $20/customer).
- Virality ≠ network effects: virality attracts off-platform people; network effects keep them. Dot-com postmortem: failures relied on price/brand effects; successes drove traffic from one user group to profit from another (Parker–Van Alstyne two-sided paper).

## 3. Monetization detail (ch.6)

- Charge for value _accrued_, at **deal completion**, not listing — performance fee skims a transaction already happening, feels negligible (Ad World: don't charge listings or membership — charge on completion; better still, sell agencies a "why you lost" postmortem = recurring revenue, zero deal friction, improves producers).
- Entry charges reduce posts → fewer potential _and realized_ interactions → less matching data. Subsidize participation instead (tools to complete profiles).
- "Users first, monetization later"; Haier exec: "You never take first money" — capture only after the value unit is created and exchanged to both sides' satisfaction. Violation kills: Murdoch promised analysts $1B Myspace revenue (real: ~$100M) → managers signed any sponsor no matter how annoying → "last nail in the coffin" (Sean Percival).
- Free-side models: razors-and-blades (Gillette story — note Picker's research undercuts the legend; printers/toner); freemium (Dropbox, MailChimp — only ~1–2% free→paid conversion, Techstars' Cohen: you need millions first); subsidize one side, charge the other (two-sided pricing theory by the authors + Tirole, cited in his 2014 Nobel).
- The value given away must be **controlled/linked to what you monetize** — Netscape gave browsers free hoping to sell servers, but Apache (free) or Microsoft servers worked just as well → nothing captured.
- Expectation-of-completeness trap — Zvents: users demanded comprehensive event listings, so threats to pull non-paying organizers' listings were toothless; premium placements barely sold → sold to eBay/StubHub. **Visitor counts ≠ monetary value; interactions must generate capturable excess value or monetization may be impossible.**
- Pricing as curation — Meetup: charging organizers $19/mo lost 95% of activity but successful meetups rose from 1–2% to ~50% — the fee weeded unserious organizers (killing negative network effects) while monetizing. Ask: can pricing strengthen positive NE _and_ reduce negative NE?
- **Transaction fee**: % or fixed (fixed suits high-frequency, uniform size). Doesn't deter joining but is dodged by taking the interaction off-platform — worst for service platforms (terms negotiated + payment after delivery = direct contact). Counters: temporarily block contact (Groupon's standardized deals; Airbnb/Fiverr ratings substitute for vetting); add value that keeps interaction on-platform (Upwork remote monitoring + pay-on-delivery; Clarity's call management, per-minute billing, invoicing — both sides gain enough to stay). Simple local services (plumbing, painting) can't own the interaction → use enhanced access instead.
- **Community access (advertising done right)**: third parties pay to reach users _without_ joining to transact — Dribbble charges companies for job listings (adds value: designers get gigs, firms get pre-curated portfolios; Dribbble refuses member fees and sponsored images as prestige-destroying). Works iff the paid content enhances the core interaction.
- **Enhanced access**: producers pay to stand out — Yelp premium listings, AdWords vs. organic SEO, Tumblr/Twitter promoted posts, dating-site pay-to-reveal-contact. Rules: paid placement must be visually distinguishable from organic (pre-Google engines that blurred it died); don't create the impression access is restricted (Facebook's organic-reach cut drew producer+consumer complaints — survived on NE strength); apply normal curation to paid content or relevance collapses.
- **Enhanced curation**: users pay for guaranteed quality — Sittercity screens babysitters rigorously → subscription instead of transaction fee; Skillshare moved from per-course fees to subscription (teachers paid royalties on subscriber enrollments).
- Whom to charge (four heuristics): charge all only when the fee itself curates (country clubs; Carbon NYC for multimillionaires — willingness-to-pay ≠ quality, use carefully); charge the side that values access more, subsidize the other (Ladies' Night; dating sites free for women); subsidize stars who attract the crowd (mall anchor leases; Microsoft conceded special terms to EA under threat of defection to Sony); subsidize the price-sensitive side — determined by scarcity, not guesses (Denver glut → owners paid brokers, tenants free; Boston scarcity → tenants paid, owners free).
- Ingenious fallback — Alibaba: couldn't track transactions (primitive software) → membership fees + >¥1M commissions to sales agents to overpower entry friction; to this day no transaction fees — monetizes via advertising.
- Free→fee transition rules: never charge for what was free (Meetup survived, Zvents didn't); never reduce accustomed access (Facebook organic reach); create _new_ value justifying the charge — and control/guarantee it (Uber's "Safe Rides" fee attacked for cutting corners on checks); **architect for monetization optionality from day one** — transaction fees need transaction capture; access fees need control of content/data flows to users. Openness choices are monetization choices.
- Don't confuse monetization with growth tools: price effects vanish when discounts end (FreePC's freeloaders); brand effects are costly and fragile (eToys; Kozmo's Whoopi Goldberg; 19 dot-com Super Bowl ads in Jan 2000 → 8 dead a decade later).

## 4. Governance detail (ch.8)

- Governance = rules on who participates, how value is divided, how conflicts resolve. Keurig 2.0: post-patent rival pods appeared → Keurig shipped a scanner blocking unbranded pods → sales fell 12%, "corporate greed" backlash, hack videos. Violated three rules: always create value for consumers; don't use power to change rules in your favor; don't take more than a fair share. It stayed a one-sided _product platform_ and excluded an ecosystem.
- Platforms as states — Facebook's 1.5B users > China's population; Google 64% US/90% EU search; Alibaba 1 trillion yuan GMV, 70% of China's commercial shipments; Burnham read Facebook Credits as _monetary policy_. Governance analogy validated by Singapore (rule of law + anti-corruption → each 1% corruption drop ≈ 1.7% GDP rise) and E/W Germany, N/S Korea.
- Market failure = good interactions fail or bad ones occur (eBay misspelling arbitrage: "Allsop's Arctic Ale" listed with one P → scavenger bought $304, resold >$78,100).
- Four failure causes → counters: **information asymmetry** (counterfeits $350B > drug trade $321B) → transparency, reputation, quality signals, insurance; **externalities** (friend sells your contact info for points; MonkeyParking privatizing public parking → shut down June 2014; positive ones like Netflix's data-driven recommendations = uncaptured value → reward public-good creation); **monopoly power of a participant** (Zynga on Facebook, eBay power sellers) → promote competitors (Facebook's 3,000 games diluted Zynga); **risk** → insurance/risk-pooling (below).
- Roth's market-design levers: safety via transparency/quality/insurance; thickness (participants find each other); minimize congestion (low quality crowding out high); minimize repugnant activity (no porn on iTunes, organs on Alibaba, child labor on Upwork).
- Lessig's four tools, deep: **laws** = explicit rules, normally transparent (Stack Overflow's public points ladder — 1 pt ask/answer, 15 upvote, 125 downvote costing you 1 pt, 200 see fewer ads — solves the public-goods problem) _except_ punitive rules: give fast open feedback for good behavior, **slow opaque feedback for bad** (dating-site stalkers learned to dodge instant flags; trolls whose posts show only to themselves stop trolling).
- **Norms**: communities are nurtured, not found — iStockphoto's founder ("Bitter") hand-inspected images, praised members publicly, then crowd curation: contributors _earned_ inspector/organizer roles by uploading quality. Behavior design loop (Eyal): trigger → action → variable reward → investment (Facebook photo → Pinterest click → curated boards → invite friends/state prefs). Norms follow Ostrom's commons rules: defined boundaries, channels to influence decisions, accountable monitors, graduated sanctions, cheap dispute resolution, nested governance tiers as scale grows.
- Norms in a rule-change — eBay fixed-price: sellers feared losing auction escalation, buyers wanted it; Jordan ran Voices program/focus groups, staged tests, pulled back failures; sided with buyers ("merchants go where the consumers are") → Buy It Now ≈70% of $83B GMV.
- **Architecture**: code as regulator — P2P lending (Zopa scored borrowers on credit + Yelp rating + email stability + LinkedIn + tool engagement; defaults 0.6%→0.2%); eBay spelling-assist killed misspelling arbitrage (disenfranchising arbitrageurs for ecosystem health — arbitrage had supplied "thickness"); IEX's timed-order matching neutralized HFT speed advantage; blockchain smart contracts (wedding photographer paid on delivery trigger — "machines hiring people"), no gatekeeper, pressure on 2–4% rake platforms.
- **Markets**: incentive design on social currency (fun, fame, fortune; reputation) — iStockphoto credit economy (download = 1 credit to each side; buy at 25¢; cash out ≥$100 → birthed microstock); SAP dev points→charity on company account ($6–8M support savings, 30-min avg response vs. 1-day SLA, ~$500K/yr partner productivity); SAP ran **expansionary monetary policy**: double points on CRM-related answers during launch → output rose.
- IP policy inside the platform — SAP: publish an 18–24-month roadmap telling devs which digital real estate stays open (a "metaphorical patent period"), plus partnering/buyouts at fair price → developers invest without fear of expropriation.
- Insurance/risk-pooling: platform owners instinctively shed participant risk early — 1960s credit-card issuers fought fraud insurance, lost to the Fair Credit Reporting Act ($50 cap) → usage rose more than fraud cost; banks now waive the $50. Same arc: Airbnb initially refused to indemnify hosts → later offered $1M protection (though secondary coverage leans on homeowners' policies); Uber initially refused rider insurance → later partnered with insurers. **Rule: pool risk for participants; don't minimize your own.**
- Governance of the platform owner (self-governance): **internal transparency** — Bezos's service-interface mandate (the "Yegge Rant": all data/functionality via interfaces, no backdoors, interfaces designed to be externalizable) → AWS (Jassy) now bigger than the next dozen clouds combined; counter-case: siloed Sony (Walkman, MP3, e-reader, PSP, movie studios) never built a platform → iPhone swept it.
- **Participation**: give external partners voice equal to internals — Intel Architecture Labs ran USB as a walled-off neutral unit (visited 50+ companies; consortium of 7: Compaq, DEC, IBM, Intel, Microsoft, NEC, Nortel). IAL's 10 self-governance rules: customers get voice via a separate unit; open standards must stay open; fair IP treatment both ways; clear roadmap + credible commitments; reserve the right to enter partner markets _with notice_; share risk/skin in the game on big investments; don't promise no change — promise early notice and shared pain; differential benefits OK if qualification rules are transparent; promote partners' (especially small ones') financial health; as the platform matures, decisions favor outward progression — core → complements → new businesses, even ones that cannibalize the platform.
- Why fairness pays: people share more ideas; and fair expected allocation → rational participation (Van Alstyne's formal result: if some firms can be expropriated, they won't join — the coalition and possibly the standard never forms).
- Governance is never finished: new value sources create new struggles; rule changes should favor the greatest new value / where the market is heading, not sunk assets (Microsoft guarding aging assets = stagnation). "Design for self-design" — governance must self-heal; spread signals of behavioral change fast.

## 5. Metrics detail (ch.9)

- Activity, not membership — BranchOut: <1M→33M signups spring–summer 2012 on viral invites, then <2M four months later; it measured enrollment while interactions were thin. Core metric: **rate of interaction success** (pipeline metrics measure value-flow efficiency; platform metrics measure creation/sharing/delivery of value across the ecosystem, on- and off-platform).
- Prefer comparative metrics — ratios/rates over absolutes (Croll & Yoskovitz, _Lean Analytics_): ratio of active to total users; growth rate of active users.
- **Stage 1 (startup) — three core metrics**: **liquidity** = minimum viable counts of producers and consumers with high % successful interactions (track % of listings that lead to interactions within a time window; also track _illiquid situations_ — Uber app showing no car); **matching quality** = search→interaction conversion (sales conversion rate; find the retention threshold — e.g., users hitting ~40% first-week interaction rate stay 3 months — then manage to it); **trust** = comfort with interaction risk, achieved by curating participants (Airbnb two-way reviews — highest review rate — + photographer-verified listings vs. Craigslist's scandals). Revenue metrics are largely irrelevant at this stage.
- Specialized stage-1 metrics: engagement per interaction, time between interactions, % active users; raw interaction counts only when value is uniform (Fiverr's $5 gigs); value proxies — Airbnb nights booked, Upwork hours delivered, Clarity call minutes; interaction capture (Amazon Marketplace gross value); content platforms — co-creation (% listings consumed), consumer relevance (% listings getting minimum positive response); market-access proxies — producer participation rate, women registered (dating), OpenTable reservations.
- **Stage 2 (growth)**: producer-to-consumer ratio among _active_ users (OkCupid throttles male visibility to balance; Upwork balances freelancers to job postings); producer metrics — participation frequency, listings created, outcomes, **interaction failure %** (initiated sales falling through — the most-overlooked metric; retained users + falling success rate = serious problem), **producer fraud** (build predictive models from repeat offenders); consumer metrics — consumption frequency, searches, conversion, repeat likelihood → producer & consumer LTV → experiment on churn; re-engagement programs (LinkedIn/Facebook pings, Twitter "popular in your network"); interaction conversion rate; **side-switching rate** (Airbnb guests → best host source).
- "Distance between consumers and producers" — Haier's metric via WeChat interactions: smaller distance → better product fit + cheaper marketing (ad budget as proxy: Google's tiny vs. Coca-Cola's huge).
- **Stage 3 (maturity)** — Tiwana's three requirements: metrics must drive innovation, have high signal-to-noise, facilitate resource allocation. Innovation sourcing: watch extension developers for missing core features and absorb them (Windows absorbed disk defrag, encryption, media players; **Cisco AXP rule via CTO Guido Jouret: embed a capability into the platform only once _multiple_ providers have independently built it across verticals — absorb too early and you cannibalize a partner's cash cow, too late and commoditization has already cut the value**); act when third-party features dominate user value (Apple Maps).
- Design rules: keep the dashboard small (oDesk "over-measured and under-prioritized" — Swart: find the one or two metrics proving customers love and use the product); kill vanity metrics (total signups rise while interactions flatten); Ries's 3-A test — **actionable** (guides decisions), **accessible** (comprehensible to those who gather it), **auditable** (real, precisely defined data); ultimate metric = happy, repeatedly engaged users on _every_ side.

## 6. Strategy detail (ch.10 + ch.11 policy angles)

- Three-dimensional chess — competition at three levels: platform vs. platform (PSP-2000 was the better device; iPhone's ecosystem crushed it — Sony stock +10% at launch, then lost), platform vs. partner (Microsoft absorbing partner innovations; Amazon selling against its own merchants — "delicate and dangerous": strengthens platform, weakens partners, long-term cost), partner vs. partner (two devs competing for the same consumers).
- Old frames weakened: five forces treats buyers/suppliers as entities to subjugate; platforms blur boundaries (student→teacher, customer→seller) — participants are partners to woo in multiple roles ("co-opetition"). Resource-based view shifts: the inimitable resource is **network access + interactions**, not owned assets (Airbnb/Uber resource pools grow faster than the firm); hypercompetition — ownership gives no moat, advantage is evanescent.
- **Prevent multihoming by limiting access** — Apple banned Flash on iOS: Jobs's "Thoughts on Flash" claimed quality/energy; real reasons — Adobe's tools ported iOS apps to Android (multihoming → commodity) and enabled in-app purchases that would cut Apple out of its 30% and the usage data. Alibaba blocked Baidu's crawlers _while starving for traffic_ — kept the shopper community closed so Alibaba (not Baidu) would own the future ad revenue; it displaced Baidu as China's top ad platform. Trade: short-term traffic for control of monetization.
- **Foster innovation, then capture its value** — roadmap windows (SAP 18–24 mo), then own the highest-value resources, cede the rest (Alibaba owns search, Facebook owns social search, Microsoft owns Office). Watch the long tail: most partner apps stay small; those that "jump rank" — especially ones growing their own interactive communities (Zynga, Instagram, Snapchat on Facebook) — get acquired (Instagram $1B; Snapchat refused $3B) or weakened via promoted competitors (Facebook's 3,000 games vs. Zynga; Zynga escaped into multihoming — QQ, iOS/Android, own cloud).
- **Leverage data**: tactical (A/B tests — Amazon's buy-button position) vs. strategic (who's creating, controlling, siphoning value on/off your platform — Facebook monitoring Zynga/Instagram). Monster vs. LinkedIn: Monster captured only active job-seekers; data flow stopped at placement. LinkedIn captured all professionals + recruiters + content loops → data supremacy becomes the platform moat (no data → no value → no interactions → no data).
- **M&A redefined**: key question is whether the target creates value for an _overlapping_ user base. Platform edge: test-drive before buying — observe the partner's real transaction data on-platform (solves M&A information asymmetry). Partner > acquire when outcomes are unpredictable (Facebook let hundreds of game firms compete rather than buy Zynga — hits flame out); arm's-length partnerships also keep the architecture modular.
- **Envelopment**: an adjacent platform serving overlapping users adds a feature → threat of multihoming/defection; respond with the same feature directly or via a partner; success = absorbing the adjacent platform's functions _and_ user base. Microsoft bundled media streaming into Windows (90% OS share → Real Audio's 100% streaming market enveloped despite superior performance). Ongoing: iPhone enveloping payments/wearables; Haier enveloping connected-home apps. Counter-envelopment is symmetric — bigger platform usually wins, but superior value can beat size (LinkedIn vs. Monster).
- **Enhanced platform design as defense** — Vimeo coexists via better hosting/bandwidth/feedback/no pre-rolls; Airbnb outgrew Craigslist on filters (searchable by quality, rooms, price, geolocation) and on-platform transactions vs. time-sorted lists and off-platform dealmaking. (The book has no literal "cherry-picking" defense; the analogous plays are long-tail monitoring + rapid capture, niche specialization, and superior design.)
- Winner-take-all — the book lists **four** forces: supply economies of scale; network effects (≈10x value multiple vs. same-revenue firms without them); high multihoming/switching costs (one phone vs. many credit cards — low multihoming costs let Facebook/LinkedIn unseat Myspace/Monster and blocked Microsoft's mobile entry); lack of niche specialization (Apple survived vs. Windows via designers/musicians; LinkedIn via professionals; Uber/Lyft has no niches → vicious poaching, alleged fake-ride sabotage).
- Policy angles that change strategy: exclusionary contracts are "especially pernicious" under strong network effects (Shapiro — risk of excess inertia) but regulators should move cautiously — Parker/Van Alstyne argue temporary category exclusivity works like a **short-term micro-patent** (SAP's preferred partners; Microsoft's game devs), accelerating innovation that later folds into the platform; predatory-pricing tests must incorporate two-sided logic — zero pricing to one side is rational, non-predatory when profits come from the other side; compatibility battles — Sun v. Microsoft over Java forking (1997 suit; 2002 suit over .NET exclusion), Android's 2015 open/proprietary split; Amazon AZW as potential Trojan-horse closed format; EU v. Google for favoring its own shopping service (FTC dropped a parallel 2013 complaint).
- Regulation can re-shore what openness destroys: Grossman's Regulation 2.0 = freedom to operate in exchange for open-data APIs and after-the-fact accountability (vs. Regulation 1.0's upfront certification).

## 7. Design detail (ch.1–4)

- Every platform exchange has three elements: **information** (always flows through the platform — the defining feature), **goods/services** (sometimes on-platform — Upwork delivery tools; sometimes off — the Uber ride), **currency** (money, or attention/reputation/influence — Twitter pays in attention, TripAdvisor/Dribbble/500px in reputation). Monetization depends on which currency flow you can internalize (eBay's ~10% cut; Facebook sells attention to advertisers).
- Pull depth: chicken-or-egg (most platforms die here); retention needs minimum connections (Facebook shifted marketing from recruiting members to helping them form connections); **single-user feedback loop** (algorithm learns your activity → better recommendations → more use) vs. **multi-user feedback loop** (Facebook news feed: producer updates → consumer likes/comments → feedback to producers → more units); pull grows with the currency's value (a tweet earns more attention on a huge Twitter); piggyback outside networks.
- Facilitate depth: producer tools (500px portfolio hosting; Quirky collaboration tools); **reduce barriers** (Instagram: snap→edit→share in 3 clicks vs. camera→PC→Photoshop→upload); sometimes **raise barriers** (Sittercity's stringent sitter screening buys trust; intrusive curation rules deter abuse — Reddit trolls, Craigslist crimes, Airbnb trashings).
- Match depth: match quality ∝ data quality (static — identity/gender/nationality; dynamic — location, status, age, search intent; Facebook's filter weighs all prior activity). Requires an explicit **data acquisition strategy**: incentives (LinkedIn profile progress bar), game elements, third-party import (Spotify's Facebook login — user resistance forced alternatives).
- Function imbalance can persist: Craigslist's pull outweighs weak facilitate/match; YouTube (pull + match) vs. Vimeo (facilitate) coexist.
- Layering new interactions (4 ways): change the value unit (LinkedIn profiles → discussion posts); add a new user category (recruiters, advertisers); add new value-unit types (UberPool/Lyft Line — ride-sharing on the taxi core); curate a subset of users into a new category (LinkedIn "thought leaders" → publishing platform). Risk case: Moodswing's amateur-therapy plan — recruiting volunteer psych students for depressed users, unlaunched and ethically fraught.
- End-to-end principle (Saltzer/Reed/Clark 1981): app-specific functions belong at the edge; only highest-volume/highest-value cross-app features enter the core. Violation = Vista "goatware" (back-compat + next-gen baked into core → unstable, dev-hostile; 2015 share: XP 12%, Vista <2%). Compliance = Jobs's Mac OS X (dumped OS 9 code, compartmentalized Classic Environment). Baldwin/Clark: a well-designed platform = **stable low-variety core + evolving high-variety periphery** (AWS optimizes a handful of ops — storage, compute, messaging — everything else to the edge).
- Modularity economics: integral architecture is faster at launch (interface documentation is real work) but blocks ecosystem mobilization; plan to re-architect (design structure matrices map dependencies — the open-source transition case under §1). Intel invested in PCI/USB interconnects so independently improving subsystems (CPU/GPU/RAM/HD) could compound.
- Anti-design: platforms emerge, not just get planned — the hashtag was user Chris Messina's invention, adopted by Twitter. Monitor behavior for unplanned value; leave room for quirks.
- Negative network effects: scale makes best-matches harder → frictionless entry must be balanced by curation. OkCupid cascade (B-level males flood A-level females → top women flee → males converge on next tier → collapse) fixed by two-level matching — interest compatibility _plus_ league-aware attractiveness filtering (win-win: fewer insults for her, more replies for him). Chatroulette: 20→1.5M users in 6 months, then the "Naked Hairy Men" problem → user filters + image-screening algorithms → regrowth. **Data-driven network effects**: more users → better curation — only if the tools are continually improved.
- Side switching lubricates scale (Uber recruits drivers from riders; Airbnb hosts from guests); both sides must scale proportionally (1 driver : ~3 riders/hr; subsidize the lagging side — Ladies' Night logic: permanent losses on side A are fine if side B profits exceed them).
- Disruption mechanics (ch.4): platforms beat pipelines on near-zero marginal cost + network effects; they reconfigure value creation (lower producer barriers — Twitter's 140 characters, Kindle self-publishing, Viki's volunteer subtitling sold to Rakuten for $200M), value consumption ("Internet-enabled intimacy" — strangers' cars/rooms/dogs), and quality control (curation that scales: Quora moved from in-house editors to algorithmic community curation); structural routes: **de-link assets from value** (the B2B platform move — trade asset _use_ independently of ownership), re-intermediation, market aggregation.
