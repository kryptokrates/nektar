# Matchmakers (Evans & Schmalensee) — deep reference

## 1. Friction taxonomy & the coordination problem

- Friction (glossary): "costs or other impediments that impede mutually advantageous interactions and exchanges" — the economist's transaction costs; the matchmaker's entire product is removing them.
- Reducing a *substantial* friction is necessary but not sufficient — ch04 thesis: Alibaba ignited on China's SME frictions while 1,600+ US B2B exchanges (Jupiter forecast $6.3T / 42% of B2B volume by 2005) were virtually all dead by ~2005 — US firms already had phones, fax, EDI, trade shows, Walmart portals, enforceable contracts.
- Value-pie mechanics: every side needs a big enough slice to stay AND the platform needs costs + return; small pies can't fund required subsidies — US exchanges couldn't pay sellers to join ("the best price for buyers is the worst price for sellers"), sellers stayed away, buyers followed.
- Coordination problem: the product sold to each side *is* access to the other side — "no product for one group if the others don't show up"; single-sided firms never face it (Chobani didn't persuade the milk to show up).
- Alibaba's five SME frictions: (1) limited geographic presence → local-market lock-in; (2) fragmented buyers/suppliers; (3) few channels to market themselves; (4) small scale → no sales/marketing resources; (5) no way to evaluate trustworthiness — 90% of online businesspeople ranked "Trust" ahead of Quality and Price.
- Trust frictions get *layered* fixes, not one fix — Alibaba: TrustPass authentication + open Feedback Forum (2001/02), Alipay escrow holding funds until buyer confirms goods (2004), managed fulfillment + QC, ratings; each layer enabled the next marketplace (B2B → Taobao → Tmall).
- Friction is context-bound: the same design wins where friction is huge and dies where institutions already handle it — China/Kenya (weak courts, no cards, sparse branches) vs US mobile payments shaving ~1s off a card swipe (Google Wallet, carrier JV, Apple Pay all struggled).
- Off-platform leakage is handled as a *governance* rule — eBay prohibits "consummating transactions outside of eBay"; congestion leaks users too, so Didi Kuaidi sells certainty (fast, confident matching) vs call-then-hail-anyway taxis.
- Survivor pattern: serve one side's standalone friction first, then network it — Ariba sold procurement software one-sided, opened ariba.com (1999) for multilateral connections, crashed $40B→$1.1B, survived on back-office value, charged both sides ~2005, sold to SAP for $4.3B (2012).

## 2. Critical mass economics

- Critical mass = the minimum *set* of participants on all sides for self-sustaining growth — a frontier of (A,B) pairs, not a magic number; many balances can ignite.
- The frontier map (fig 5-2): NE = "growth zone" (enough of both sides → users stay, others join); SW = "implosion zone" (too few of either → the other leaves, no one joins → death spiral). Every platform starts at (0,0) and must cross before time or money runs out.
- Frontier distance varies: it sits nearer the origin when one side values even a few of the other side highly, or when moderate counts suffice — high per-match value or standalone value lowers the bar.
- Momentum is the real clock: the longer below mass, the more the platform reads as a "dead spot" — users stop coming, the dud reputation hardens, investor patience thins.
- Thickness ≠ headcount: need the *right* participants in the right proportions — diners wanted ≥a dozen restaurant choices per time/cuisine/area; a Seattle table is worthless to an SF diner (50 thin cities < 4 deep ones).
- Negative direct effects cap useful size: same-side congestion/competition destroys value (more men at the singles bar hurts each man) — sometimes limit a side to keep it attractive.
- Reversibility varies: locked-in hardware standards (fax, consoles) rarely lose members; low-commitment platforms lose them constantly (Friendster→MySpace→Facebook); multihoming lets late entrants squeeze in — Discover got consumers to carry +1 card, merchants to accept +1 brand.
- First-mover / winner-take-all is shaky: Visa went national 16 yrs after the first card network; most first movers died (sixdegrees.com); differentiation overturns leads (Facebook's gated, cleaner environment); "winner takes most" at best, transient.
- Turbocharge accelerants (ch03): chips, Internet, web, fixed+mobile broadband, languages/OSes, Cloud → *foundational platforms* ("platforms-for-platforms": ISPs link users↔edge providers; OSes link users↔devs) hand new matchmakers global reach at ~zero marginal cost — Uber entered Brussels needing only drivers + lawyers.
- When mass is unreachable: the pie can't fund required subsidies (US B2B); the design thins every market instead of thickening one (Apple Pay's go-broad/go-shallow); the clock runs out before momentum compounds (Brightcove). Authors' window: ~a couple of years, then implosion or capital cutoff.
- Severity depends on whether sides are distinct groups or the same people in different roles — M-PESA's senders and receivers were mostly the same users at different times, softening the puzzle; YouTube's uploaders/viewers overlapped but had to be courted as distinct roles.

## 3. Ignition strategies & tactics

- Three strategies (ch05). *Zigzag*: push all sides alternately/simultaneously — YouTube (upload features one month, viewer features the next, until feedback took over); Alibaba signed suppliers + buyers at once. *Two-step*: secure one side, sell access to it — OpenTable restaurants→diners; Brightcove publishers first (died on step two); ad models bait eyeballs then sell them (YouTube's first ad 28 months post-launch; Pinterest ~50). *Commitment*: guarantee the other side shows when a side must invest to play — Microsoft's low Xbox price promised game devs an installed base; Android's free open license let handset makers commit without trusting Google.
- Self-supply a side — YouTube founders uploaded "Me at the zoo" (and offered $100/10 videos on Craigslist; no takers); Apple shipped its own apps pre-App Store; Palm bundled apps then opened. Breaks the wait for the other side.
- Marquee customers — anchor tenants get below-market rent *because* their traffic is what smaller tenants pay for; OpenTable chased top Zagat-list restaurants per city; nightclubs pack opening night with "cool" people for buzz.
- Shape expectations ("make 'em believe") — entrepreneur credibility (Levchin's PayPal record won Affirm's capital partners + merchants), conditional contracts (join-if-enough-others), or bluntly overstating recruitment progress.
- Go narrow/deep to manufacture thickness — OpenTable abandoned 50 thin cities for 4; PayPal ignored all web shoppers/merchants and became the standard inside eBay's P2P traffic first (78% of volume off-eBay by Q2 2015 — the beachhead enabled the expansion); Diners Club seeded wealthy Upper East Siders + 14 upscale Manhattan restaurants (1950), then replicated city-by-city.
- Piggyback an existing network — YouTube embeds on MySpace/blogs + email links turned every share into recruiting; M-PESA converted Safaricom airtime shops into 307 launch CICO agents across all 70 districts; AmEx's travel offices + cardholder base powered its 1958 counter to Diners Club; the 1966 MasterCard/Visa associations gave every member bank instant access to both sides, letting even small banks ignite.
- Exclusivity-for-control — Apple's per-country carrier deals (AT&T, 4 yrs) traded reach (26.2% of US subs) for total handset control: no carrier apps, no blocked features.
- Design the feedback loop directly — YouTube's related videos, comments, ratings, "recently viewed," featured lists raised the odds uploaders and viewers interacted; features, not ads, drove the Nov 2005–Mar 2006 ignition spike.
- Fizzle anatomy — Brightcove (well-funded, ex-Macromedia CTO): step one worked but premium publishers wouldn't feed a consumer destination competing for their own eyeballs; no UGC long tail; consumer site closed Apr 2008. Cohort die-off: Google Video closed 2012; MySpace Video/MSN/Yahoo marginal — five sites held ~92% of traffic in May 2006, only YouTube crossed. eBay China withdrew — critical mass is per-market, not global.

## 4. Pricing

- Two decisions: price *level* (P_T = P_A + P_B) and price *structure* (each side's share) — structures are usually tilted: WEX's ~$2.00/transaction split $1.50 truck stop / $0.50 fleet.
- The >100% structure: a negative price on one side means the money side pays >100% of the total — OpenTable restaurants effectively pay >100% since diners pay nothing and get rewards.
- Four sensitivities, not one: each side's own price sensitivity + each side's sensitivity to the other's participation — no market-research shortcut; judgment required.
- Prices compound through feedback: raise A's price → fewer A → platform worth less to B → fewer B → worth less to A; integrating the loop yields the below-cost/free/negative optimum.
- Who pays — the deep rules: (a) less price-sensitive side pays more (advertisers tolerate pay-per-performance; web readers flee small charges); (b) the needier side pays — "desperate to meet" funds "nice to meet" (ladies' night; Rosebar's paying-men VIP room + invited women); (c) the side that *controls whether the interaction happens* gets priced low/subsidized — fleets decide card usage → pay $0.50 of $2.00; cardholders decide the swipe → AmEx pays rewards; searchers choose the engine → free (Bing even pays rewards).
- Access vs usage fees are separate levers: access monetizes the option (AmEx annual fee; OpenTable's $199/mo license; LSE membership), usage monetizes the interaction ($1/seated diner; ~3% merchant discount). Pair high access + zero/negative usage to encourage the activity the other side pays for; skip usage fees when monitoring is costly/annoying (WSJ flat access).
- Don't charge access fees to a side whose mere presence creates positive indirect effects — merchants join AmEx free, shoppers enter malls free.
- Discount big participants for *network contribution*, not just volume — large truck-stop chains pay less per transaction because their coverage makes the network valuable to all fleets; same logic as anchor-tenant rent breaks.
- In-kind subsidy = content as bribe — media math: Jack pays $20 to reach Annie, platform charges $15, gives Annie $10 of content, keeps $5.
- Lifecycle rebalancing (the "moving train"): fleet cards went fleet-pays (1970s Comdata checks) → flat (1990s) → tilted (post-2000 fuel squeeze + truck-stop consolidation → stops now ~75% of revenue); newspapers' reader share ≤20% for a decade → >33% by 2013. Launch pricing doesn't freeze the optimum, but ignition pricing usually must persist through the crossing.
- Charge both sides when neither needs subsidizing (WSJ readers+advertisers; nightclubs both sexes; dating sites same price) — subsidy exists only where participation won't come at a positive price.
- Mature categories dictate structure (new malls can't charge shoppers; online pubs can't charge readers); pioneers have no model — OpenTable/Alibaba got it right by judgment or luck. Design precedes pricing: pick side count, or whether to be a platform at all (Zappos reverted to one-sided for control; Jobs launched iPhone single-sided).

## 5. Openness & exclusivity

- Design large vs design small — Monster admits all employers/seekers; specialized recruiters prescreen and charge more: fewer participants, better matches, higher value per participant. No universal answer.
- Screening devices (glossary): *exclusionary vibes* (FarmersOnly — "city folks just don't get it"), *exclusionary amenities* (Hoist's crane-operator content pulls the audience its advertisers want), price/review gates (LSE membership fee; App Store review).
- Limit a side to protect it from itself: same-side competition repels that side (B2B exchanges promised buyers lowest prices — exactly why sellers refused); curating competitors can recruit them.
- Marquee exception: some same-side participants net-benefit their own rivals via traffic — anchor tenants get rent breaks; clubs fast-track attractive patrons.
- Congestion caps size: physical limits force entry restriction (fire code, mingling room); the alternative is shrinking the wait via better search/matching (ride apps vs taxi uncertainty; sparse results pages).
- Exclusivity as ignition: Apple's carrier exclusives bought freedom from carrier meddling — at the price of excluding 73.8% of US subscribers at launch.
- Exclusivity as risk: pre-2007 carrier walled gardens demanded per-carrier permission + per-handset builds — "the software industry for mobile phones … one of the most dysfunctional in all technology."
- Openness as ecosystem strategy: Android's free open license killed the ~20%-of-handset OS fee and let anyone fork (a commitment device) — but created fragmentation risk via the collective-action problem (industry wants one standard, each firm a variant); the 34-member OHA + certification kept standard-Android apps running everywhere.
- Sequence openness: iPhone launched closed (Jun 2007), opened to devs ~13 months later (App Store Jul 2008); Facebook accreted sides over a decade to reach six.
- A would-be side can refuse to exist: no OEM would build Xbox consoles — consoles are the subsidy side — so Microsoft self-manufactured.
- Architectures: vertically integrated platform (Windows + Microsoft apps competing with devs; Office out-earned Windows 2013); hybrid reseller (Amazon retail + Marketplace, >40% units 3rd-party by 2014); pure platform (eBay); "little bit single" (Xbox Studios games) / "little bit multi" (Tencent hosts third-party games). Single-sided (Zappos, Netflix) buys experience control at the price of inventory, capital, operating cost.

## 6. Operating balance & mature platforms

- Thickness is engineered: exchanges calibrate *tick size* to pay market makers for inventory risk (wider tick = richer market-making, costlier trading); signaling devices (Visa card bug + acceptance mark) let strangers recognize a valid match; standards collapse interaction friction (Twitter's 140 chars, SDKs).
- "Search diversion" is deliberate interior design — Aventura maximizes walking past storefronts (split escalators, end-placed anchors); Vogue buries the TOC in ads; a small priced-in annoyance funds the match.
- Placement balances agglomeration vs competition — Tiffany opposite Cartier: shared traffic outweighs rivalry; decided with the anchors, who hold the bargaining power.
- Governance economics (ch09): *behavioral* externalities — what participants do, not how many — can sink a platform; governance = rules + detection + punishment, the platform's own legal system. When real law can't help (options trading was unenforceable gambling under Barnard's Act 1734), platform law is the product: Jonathan's Coffee House ejected fraudsters → public blackboard → LSE (1801) paid members + committee + expulsion.
- Ejection is the ultimate sanction — OpenTable terminates at 4 no-shows/yr; Facebook's ~150 User Ops staff (~1/5 of headcount) enforce real names/conduct (Lohan expelled); "Google Jail" buried JCPenney on page 7 ~90 days for link-farm SEO.
- Ratings distribute enforcement — Uber/BlaBlaCar two-sided ratings let each side filter the other; BlaBlaCar adds ID verification, photos, ladies-only.
- Gate screens prevent lemons — App Store rejects "boring"/derivative apps so quality devs aren't "surrounded by amateur hour"; screening limits size but raises the other side's willingness to pay.
- Governance philosophy is a competitive weapon with P&L consequences — Friendster's fakester purges pushed users to MySpace; MySpace's "everything goes" won users but repelled the money side (brands refused sordid pages → remnant ad networks → seedier vibe); Facebook's staged .edu gating built a "real people" culture holding both sides.
- Post-ignition platforms layer sides: M-PESA added bill pay, airtime, M-Shwari savings/loans (KShs 24B lent by early 2015), Lipa Na M-PESA merchant payments — four intersecting two-sided platforms anchored on the registered user.
- Feedback runs both ways at maturity — Owings Mills lost Boscov's → traffic fell → stores left → "dead mall" (deadmalls.com; ~1 in 5 US malls ≥10% vacant by 2014); newspapers: ads fell → content cut → readers left → ad appeal fell further.

## 7. The six-question checklist — full depth (ch10)

1. *What's the friction, how big, who benefits?* Bigger friction → bigger pie → easier recruitment + subsidy budget + profit room; *who* benefits most steers ignition order and price structure. Two species: reducing a known friction (OpenTable — does the pie cover costs?) vs enabling an impossible interaction (YouTube — value unsizable pre-launch). Authors' #1 mistake: "if you build it, they will come" — no friction, no case.
2. *Does the design reduce the friction, balance all sides, beat other entrants?* Usually no — every entrant sees the same friction (a crowd of video sites launched with YouTube); survival turns on better design/price/ecosystem play, not the idea.
3. *How hard is ignition, and is there a real plan?* Right participants in right proportions before money runs out or the dud reputation sets; novices underrate the hardest problem in the model.
4. *Do ignition/growth prices let it make money?* If subsidies are required, demand the story: why the money side pays enough to fund them + profit, and why those prices won't cap money-side growth. Ad models have it easiest — a developed market already buys eyeballs.
5. *How will it work with the ecosystem, what risks, has it dealt with them?* Map outside-the-walls dependencies: Apple and Google each engineered a carrier strategy; Brightcove never reckoned its media partners would see its consumer site as competition.
6. *Can the entrepreneur modify strategy fast on feedback?* Initial plans always need correction — OpenTable repriced (purchase → install fee + rental) when restaurants balked, then restrategized (eyeballs → narrow/deep). "Skateboard rider": constant small adjustments.
- The countdown: launch starts the clock — most ignite within ~2 years or never (OpenTable needed ~3 + a wholesale strategy change; ~6 to real takeoff).
- Warning signs: can't land even forgiving early adopters (YouTube's dead first months → redesign); marquee players won't sign (OpenTable's Zagat-list closes were the tell); participants/activity stop accelerating; early adopters defect; no "takeoff" inflection after a year or two → implosion or capital cutoff.
- Scored case — Apple Pay yr 1: friction weak (US swipe already fast); design thinned both sides (needed iPhone 6 *and* NFC terminals — ~65% of adults, ~77% of top-100 retailers couldn't play); 9.1% of eligibles activated, 4.6% used-when-able (Black Friday); a year in: 16.6% ever-tried, use-when-able fell to 30.7% — ~0.18% of card transactions. Worried, not dead: patient capital buys a slow fuse — contrast Starbucks QR (2011): ran on existing phones/readers → 2% of transactions yr 1, 16% by 2014.

## 8. Ecosystem risk — beyond the castle walls (ch07)

- Ecosystem = all people/businesses/institutions/things affecting the value a platform can create (mall's roads, bus routes, local affluence; Windows' OEMs, devs, mouse-pad makers). Dependence is mutual: a strong mall lifts neighbors; a flop (Windows 8) hurts everyone.
- Choose inside vs outside the walls — and don't wait for the ecosystem to fill a gap you can fill: OpenTable wrote the table-management software nobody was building, which doubled as the restaurant-recruitment wedge.
- Ecosystem actors hold veto power: pre-2007 walled gardens meant per-carrier permission + per-handset builds; one carrier imposed "10,000 requirements" on Symbian; an Orange dev couldn't ship an app threatening 30-yr-old legacy services.
- Ownership/governance structure determines ecosystem power: Symbian — owned by rival handset makers demanding custom UIs, beholden to carriers for ~90% of distribution — couldn't ship global releases or standard APIs; it made the ecosystem *more* dysfunctional and fizzled despite ~60% smartphone share (2004–08), 78.7M units (2007).
- Two canonical fixes: Apple bought *control* (carrier exclusives → no carrier apps, no feature blocks); Google bought *allegiance* (free license + OHA + certification + $10M app contest + ported Google apps + carriers got most app-distribution commissions). Same principles, opposite postures — both broke the gardens.
- Partner-as-competitor conflict: Brightcove's premium publishers wouldn't feed a consumer site competing for their own ad eyeballs — an ecosystem misread that killed step two.
- Deep pockets can't buy every leg: Apple Pay organized networks + banks (0.15% bounty, joint promotion) but couldn't move merchants — NFC stayed ~10% of retail a year in.
- Foundational platforms are leverage *and* dependence: ISPs/OSes give billions of reach cheaply (Uber's expansion kit = drivers + lawyers) but can gate you — hardware (NFC), store review, SIM capability all sit outside your walls.
- Incumbent playbook under invasion (ch12): milk-and-exit (Yellow Pages vs search); pivot on existing two-sided assets (AmEx 1958 answered Diners Club); route around structural limits via association (1966 MasterCard/Visa let regulation-bound banks share both sides); or die refusing (Blockbuster laughed off Netflix in 2000 — 18,739 US video stores in 2005, 83% gone by 2013).
