# Obviously Awesome (April Dunford) — deep reference

## 1. The 10-step positioning process

Sequence matters: alternatives → attributes → value → segment → frame → trend. Starting anywhere else produces positioning that doesn't match how customers evaluate.

- **Step 1 — Understand the customers who love you.** Make a short list of _best-fit_ customers: bought quickly, became raving fans, refer you, act as references. Survey only the ecstatic — polling all customers gives muddy, patternless results; the fans reveal why you win. Everything downstream anchors to this list.
  - _No happy customers yet?_ Keep positioning loose — the "fishing net" principle: position broadly as a fish net, watch what you catch (grouper vs tuna), tighten only once a pattern emerges.
  - _Trap:_ don't start from product features — that unconsciously anchors you to the competitors _you_ track (rival startups customers never heard of). Positioning is built on the customer's frame, not yours.
  - _Investor positioning ≠ customer positioning._ Investors buy a 5–10yr vision; customers buy immediate pain relief. "Disruption" excites investors and frightens customers (implies ripping out their investments). Sales deck and pitch deck may diverge completely.
  - _Product vs company:_ single-product companies should fuse product and company brand (Slack, Basecamp, Twilio, Box; Crocs hit $1B on one product; Salesforce was single-product 10+ yrs). Multi-product: position the entry/revenue-dominant product first; company positioning is the umbrella.
- **Step 2 — Form a positioning team.** Cross-functional by necessity — sales, marketing, CS, product each see different alternatives, attributes and best-fit traits. The business owner (CEO/founder, or BU leader) must visibly drive it; an exercise not led by the business leader fails. Marketing initiates but cannot "own" positioning — outputs touch messaging, targeting, account strategy, onboarding, roadmap. 1–2 senior people per function (worked with 3–12 people); use an outside facilitator to challenge cast-in-stone assumptions.
- **Step 3 — Align vocabulary, drop baggage.** Spend the first hour on shared definitions (what positioning is, the components, how market maturity changes style). Then surface positioning baggage: where does product history show up in current positioning — original market, original terminology? Team must explicitly agree the product may no longer belong in the market it was conceived for, and suspend old opinions. Founders carry the most baggage; customers carry none.
  - _Arm & Hammer:_ baking soda → fridge deodorizer as baking declined; $16M (1969) → $318M (1987).
  - _Clearpath Robotics:_ positioned as "robots" → prospects compared them to stationary manufacturing robots. Repositioned as "self-driving vehicles" (OTTO Motors division, headlights/taillights, car-style site) → mobility/dispatch/fleet strengths obvious; sales accelerated, $30M raise.
- **Step 4 — List true competitive alternatives.** The foundation step. Ask of best-fit customers: _what would you do if we didn't exist?_ — not "what problem do you have" (customers describe problems badly: her DBAs said "run a fast query" but named BI tools/data warehouses as alternatives, revealing the real category). Answers are often "hire an intern", "Excel + manual process", "do nothing". In B2B software the most common alternative is general-purpose tools + manual process.
  - Stay on best-fit customers' alternatives; rank most→least common; ignore tiny competitors with no mindshare ("would customers really use them?"). Cluster into 2–5 groups (e.g., "do it manually": intern + DIY-in-Excel).
- **Step 5 — Isolate unique attributes.** Features/capabilities you have that alternatives lack — attributes, not value yet. List broadly, even apparent negatives (onsite-required install: sales saw a negative, CS saw high-touch differentiator). Include non-product attributes: delivery model (on-prem vs SaaS), business model (Rent the Runway leasing), expertise, partnerships, channels.
  - _Provability filter:_ "outstanding service" and "easy to use" are benefits masquerading as features — every company claims them. Keep only objective, provable attributes; third-party quotes, analyst statements, certifications, headcounts count as proof.
  - _Consideration vs retention attributes:_ position on what wins the evaluation (consideration), not what drives renewal (support quality) — prospects never stay long enough to experience retention attributes if positioning didn't win the deal.
- **Step 6 — Map attributes to value themes.** Chain: feature → benefit → value (= benefit expressed as a customer goal). "15-megapixel camera" → sharper images → "photos stay sharp printed or zoomed"; "all-metal construction" → "save $50k/yr on frame replacements"; "one-click reports" → "every level of the org knows the status of key metrics". A feature maps to several value points; combos of features can map to one.
  - Technical people conflate feature and benefit — force the "so what?" chain: patented fuzzy-logic algorithm → faster response → "answer your customer while they're still on the phone instead of calling back" → higher satisfaction.
  - Cluster value into 1–4 _themes_ from the customer's perspective ("works offline" + "any device" → "supports remote environments"). One theme is fine; goal is what goes front-and-center, not a feature inventory.
- **Step 7 — Determine who cares a lot.** Actionable segmentation beyond demo/firmographics ("small business" is not a segment). Capture easily identifiable characteristics that predict caring: for businesses — how they sell, other products they've invested in, skills they lack in-house (a QuickBooks-integration feature lands hardest on firms sending many invoices). Keep asking "who cares and why?" per value theme until the profile is listable — you could build a named prospect list (banks, Pokémon fans — yes; "companies that value design" — no).
  - Best-fit vs rest: best-fit buy fast, don't haggle, may say you underprice, tell friends, fight churn; the rest like you but jump to cheaper/better.
  - Target as narrowly as needed to hit _near-term_ sales goals; broaden later. Segment must (a) be big enough to meet goals, (b) share a specific, important, unmet need. Positioning evolves — it doesn't have to fit your 5-year self.
- **Steps 8–9** — see §2–3.
- **Step 10 — Capture positioning.** Detailed doc per component + one-page _positioning canvas_: product + one-line description; market category (+subcategory); competitive alternatives ("what would customers use if you didn't exist"); unique attributes; value; "who cares a lot" characteristics. The canvas exists so marketing, sales and product consume the same source.

## 2. Market-frame options in full

- **Finding candidate markets:** (a) abductive reasoning — which category's products typically have these features / deliver this value; (b) adjacent _growing_ markets — overlaps/blurry lines where your value looks at home (only pick if it makes strengths obvious; wanting in isn't enough); (c) ask customers — cautiously: they slot you into markets adjacent to your current one, or the most obvious market — rarely the best.
- **Head to Head.** Win the whole existing market on its current definition and buying criteria — buyers already know the game (cola = brown, fizzy, sweet; laptops = screen/weight/RAM/price).
  - _Use when:_ you're the leader (status quo suits you — reinforce criteria, crush redefinition attempts); or the category is understood but has _no established leader_ (smart glasses — North could lead without creating a category). Small/new player vs incumbent: almost never ("out-cola Coke") — exception: big challenger plus shifting sands (new capability, regulation, economic shift).
  - _Costs:_ no need to teach the category, but you must fit its existing definition and win on established criteria with hard evidence; challengers fight two fronts — proving superiority and blocking rivals redefining criteria. Leaderless-category entrants must move fast to claim leadership (often means raising money — bad fit for bootstrappers).
  - _Example:_ her startup as "database" = declared war on Oracle's criteria, lost every meeting ("how are you better than Oracle?"). As "data warehouse" — real demand, no leader — Oracle comparisons stopped, strengths centered, premium pricing (databases were commodity-priced).
- **Big Fish, Small Pond.** Win a definable subsegment of an existing category whose needs the leader underserves — "Coke for dogs": borrow the category's assumptions, change one rule. Still must be good-enough on general criteria; the pitch is the unmet subsegment need.
  - _Use when:_ category well-understood + clear leader (not you) + identifiable/listable subsegment (industry, geography, preference, ecosystem — "developers certified on Microsoft tools", "Mac users") with an _important, specific_ unmet need + you meet it far better than the leader.
  - _Advantages:_ homogeneous targets → easy lists, easy reach; tight communities share information → word-of-mouth; each win references the next; traction accelerates in-segment. Then expand to adjacent segments (credit unions → retail banks → insurance), eventually maybe head-on vs the leader.
  - _Risks:_ segment must still meet near-term revenue goals; leaders can swat with "good enough" responses or acquire a blocker — pick an advantage hard to copy (IP, or copying damages their core market — Coke can't court dogs without hurting the human brand).
  - _Failure mode — fake unmet need:_ Bic for Her — same pen, pink; mocked in reviews, withdrawn. A demographic isn't a need.
  - _Example:_ Janna Systems — "cheaper, crappier Siebel" in enterprise CRM → repositioned "CRM for investment banks" (its relationship-modeling feature solved a revenue problem only bankers felt) → bank-specific demos/terms, eliminated Siebel in deals, raised prices, $2M→$70M in 18 months, acquired $1.7B. (Salesforce similarly stayed SMB rather than fight Siebel head-on.)
- **Create a New Game.** Define a category where none exists, set its boundaries and buying criteria around your strengths, lead it.
  - _Use only when:_ no existing category frames your value, or you're big enough to educate the market. Preconditions: a massive shift (technology, regulation, economy) makes it possible; product is _demonstrably, inarguably_ new — not wishful positioning; a strong "why now?" answer.
  - _Work:_ sell the problem first (customers don't know it exists or what it costs them), then teach evaluation criteria, then establish yourself as leader — the most teaching of any style. Expect adjacent leaders to claim you're "merely a feature" — prepare counter-arguments.
  - _Costs/risks:_ slowest, most expensive; needs patient, deep pockets. Classic failure: you build category understanding, then a bigger or fast-follower steals leadership at the moment it clicks. Reward: the market is tailored to your strengths and you define the criteria — durable leadership.
  - _Example:_ Eloqua — "demand generation automation" (investors/analysts baffled, market looked tiny) → "marketing automation" as demand-gen buyers went mainstream → $12M→$96M, IPO, $870M Oracle acquisition. Mark Organ: real categories are _discovered_ from "freaks" — extreme early customers — not invented at offsites.
- _Adjacent-company reposition:_ Wattpad — story platform → "global multi-platform entertainment company" (Wattpad Studios), monetizing story data for TV/film after a Philippine TV hit proved the model.

## 3. Trends — rules of engagement

- Aim for the intersection of three circles: your strengths ∩ your market context ∩ a trend your _customers_ care about. Trend answers "why now?", ties you to a company priority → more strategic, urgent purchase.
- Trend ≠ category. "AI" alone tells buyers nothing; "AI-empowered CRM" works — category says what it is, trend says why it matters now. (Pantone: a pink couch is still a couch.)
- Failure modes: (a) trend with no market — "cool but baffling" ("sharing economy for pets", "Uber for cats"; the actual product was a pet-services marketplace); (b) trend + market but no link to _your product_ — Long Island Iced Tea → "Long Blockchain": stock spike, no strategy, delisted; (c) content drift — blogging about the trend so hard you forget to sell.
- Optional, never required. "Better a little boring than completely baffling" — successful boring companies exist; successful bewildering ones don't.
- _Worked examples:_ Redgate (database tools, already category leader) — wove "database DevOps" into positioning + content + sales enablement → +100% inbound leads, more multi-product deals. Sampler — "online product sampling" confused brands → "direct-to-consumer sampling" rode the DTC trend brands feared (Dollar Shave, Amazon) → faster closes (L'Oréal, Unilever).

## 4. Positioning statement → sales narrative

- **The traditional template** — "FOR _target buyers_, _offering_ IS A _market category_ WHICH PROVIDES _benefit_ UNLIKE _competitor_ WHICH PROVIDES _benefit_" — and why it fails: assumes you already know the answers; reinforces default/status-quo thinking; produces unmemorable mumbo-jumbo nobody ever reuses (her IBM DB2 statement was "malicious compliance"). Sole value: its blanks enumerate the real components.
- **The sales story arc** — the agreed skeleton a sales deck is later built from (define the story as a team; don't write copy as a team):
  1. Problem definition — bounds the discussion ("insurers must simplify claims for digital-savvy customers").
  2. How customers solve it today + where current solutions fall short.
  3. "The perfect world" — what an ideal solution's features would be.
  4. Introduce the product _in its market category_ ("Mobileclaimsorama is a mobile claims management solution for insurers").
  5. Walk each value theme → how the product enables it.
  6. Proof: case study, customer list, objection handling.
  7. The ask / next step.
     The real output is team agreement on problem, alternatives, gap, and the purchase criteria buyers should apply.
- **Messaging:** solo marketing work downstream of the story. Maintain _one master messaging document_ as the baseline — without it, copy builds on modified copy and both messaging and positioning drift.

## 5. Positioning vs messaging vs branding vs go-to-market

- Positioning is upstream business strategy — "deliberately defining how you are the best at something that a defined market cares a lot about"; input to every marketing/sales tactic, not a marketing artifact. Flow: positioning → sales story → messaging → campaigns/web/sales materials.
- Messaging = expression of positioning per medium; drifted messaging = drifted positioning (hence the master doc).
- Positioning also drives: roadmap (an "accounting software" roadmap ≠ a "financial services" roadmap — Wave renamed and rebuilt around finance after repositioning), pricing (categories carry price expectations — raising "CRM for investment banks" prices _reinforced_ the category), CS onboarding/expansion, sales segmentation/account strategy.
- Brand: single-product company → one brand for product+company (fewer things to remember); multi-product → company brand is the umbrella over per-product positions.

## 6. When/how to reposition

- **Signals positioning is wrong** (intro): happy customers, confused prospects; meetings spent explaining, not closing — prospects invent a position for you. Test: ask prospects and happy customers separately to describe what you do; a disconnect = positioning problem. Also: long cycles/low close rates with late-stage dropouts; high early churn + demand for features you won't build (mis-sold buyers recovering sunk cost — worst case, the roadmap gets hijacked by soon-to-churn customers while best-fit needs wait); price pressure (undifferentiated = can't charge a premium).
- **Revisit triggers** (ch. 13): every ~6 months, or on a major event — credible competitor enters (esp. via acquisition that adds reach); regulation shifts (privacy/security waves); economic swings (reframe revenue-growth value ↔ cost-savings); new tech changes what's possible (3G→LTE made mobile video real → a video feature became a differentiator); buyer preferences shift (CIO enthusing over a startup's purple server lights — within a year every hardware vendor talked "design").
- **Competitor-entry test:** change only if _target customers_ expect the newcomer to matter to them (or to shift prices/features). If not, hold.
- **How:** restart at Step 4 — has the alternatives list shifted in best customers' minds? Then re-derive attributes → value → segment → frame.
- Root causes: Trap 1 — product drifted from intent (cake→muffin, database→data warehouse); Trap 2 — market shifted around a static product ("diet muffin" → "gluten-free paleo snack").

## 7. Common positioning failures (anti-patterns)

- **Default positioning:** assuming only one position exists — never chosen deliberately ("cake on a stick" = Frankencake; repositioned as "cake pop", the stick suddenly makes sense).
- **Template-as-process:** the positioning statement exercise (§4).
- **"For everyone" targeting:** broader focus = weaker connection and harder closes; luke-warm customers churn on price.
- **Unprovable attribute positioning:** "easy to use", "great service" — your opinion isn't proof; only customers/reviewers/data are.
- **Feature-first positioning:** starting from what you built instead of the customer's alternatives — anchors to competitors buyers never heard of.
- **Category mismatch:** the "email for lawyers" product — no calendar (email-category assumptions), real value was secure file sharing → repositioned as team collaboration.
- **Category where you can't win the criteria:** "database" vs Oracle — every meeting lost to "how are you better than Oracle?"
- **Fake subsegment:** Bic for Her — a demographic, not an unmet need.
- **Hype positioning:** Long Blockchain — trend glued on with no product link.
- **Premature category creation:** new game attempted without a market shift, without demonstrable difference, or without resources to teach — or teaching the market then losing leadership to a fast follower.
- **Surveying the average:** polling all customers equally instead of the ecstatic minority — no pattern emerges.
- **Positioning for investors:** vision-led ("disruption", TAM) framing in customer-facing materials.

## 8. Developer tools / technical products / open source

Book's context is B2B tech throughout (IBM DB2, database→data warehouse, Janna CRM, Clearpath robots, Redgate database tools) — the method is built for complex, salesperson-mediated cycles. Directly applicable points:

- **Engineers conflate feature and benefit** — the megapixel exception: phone buyers were _trained_ to translate megapixels→quality; your spec has no such training behind it, so the feature→benefit→value chain must be explicit (fuzzy-logic algorithm → answer while the customer's still on the phone).
- **Attributes beyond features** suit devtools/platform products: delivery model (on-prem vs SaaS — self-hosted vs hosted is an attribute), business model, unique expertise, partnerships, integrations ("integrates with QuickBooks", "one-click installation" are her example attributes). Patents/unique algorithms are valid but need third-party proof.
- **Competitive alternatives for technical products** follow the same shape: DIY/manual/general-purpose tools — the devtool analog of "hire an intern" is "script it ourselves" / "use the OSS thing and glue it". Rank by what _best-fit users_ would actually do.
- **Technical buyers still buy on context:** purple-lights server anecdote — even deeply technical buyers respond to non-functional differentiation once category norms shift.
- **Founders/PhD teams carry the heaviest baggage** (her database team never questioned "we're a database"). The deeper the technical identity, the harder Step 3.
- **Closest devtools case:** Redgate — 800k-user database-tools leader made a "boring" category urgent by layering the DevOps trend (§3).
- **Users-as-contributors / OSS is not addressed** — no community, open-core, or bottom-up-adoption discussion exists in the book. Nearest usable material: tight subsegments share information → word-of-mouth compounds inside communities; "who cares a lot" characteristics can include ecosystem traits (tools already adopted, certifications held).
